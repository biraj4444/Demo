Case study

Expressive Send

What if sending a message could feel like sending something to another person? This is the reasoning behind a small interaction prototype, and what it would take to turn it into a real feature.

The concept

Expressive Send adds one small beat to the ordinary act of sending a message. Instead of a bubble simply appearing in the thread, a paper plane launches from the composer, follows a smooth curved path with a light, natural zig-zag, reaches the recipient's avatar, produces a brief expressive effect, and only then does the message settle into the conversation.

The animation is deliberately short and restrained. It is meant to add a moment of warmth to a routine action, not to slow the conversation down or draw attention to itself on repeat use.

How it started

The idea began outside of messaging entirely. While experimenting with interactive animations for a personal portfolio, an earlier project used a paper plane as a delivery-style visual — something departing one point and arriving at another. Watching that animation land raised an obvious question: the same visual idea could represent a message travelling from one person to another, rather than a package.

That question turned into this prototype: a working chat interface where sending a message triggers the plane, rather than just describing the idea in words or static mockups.

Interaction flow

1

Type a message, same as usual.

2

Press send — the bubble doesn't appear right away.

3

A small paper plane lifts off from the composer.

4

It flies toward the recipient on a smooth, slightly curved path.

5

It reaches their avatar and produces a brief arrival effect.

6

The message appears in the thread.

Design decisions

  • A curved flight path with a slight, natural zig-zag rather than a straight line, so the plane reads as gliding rather than sliding.
  • Controlled rotation along the path, so the plane's nose follows its direction of travel instead of spinning freely.
  • A single, brief arrival effect at the recipient's avatar — enough to register as an event, short enough not to linger.
  • The message bubble appears immediately after arrival, so the animation never delays the actual content of the conversation.
  • Built mobile-first, since messaging is primarily a mobile experience.
  • Respects prefers-reduced-motion, collapsing the animation to a near-instant transition for anyone who has that setting enabled.

Product considerations

Performance

The flight runs on transform and opacity changes driven by requestAnimationFrame, which keeps it light, but it hasn't been profiled against low-end devices or a production message list.

Accessibility

Reduced motion is respected at the CSS level. It hasn't yet been tested with screen readers, and the moment when the message becomes available to assistive technology needs its own design pass.

Repeated messages

Sending several messages quickly is untested territory — whether each one should get its own flight, queue, or a simplified treatment after the first is an open question.

Device compatibility

Built and tested primarily on a single Android device in a mobile browser. Tablet, foldable, and older-device behavior hasn't been verified.

Prototype limitations

This is an interaction prototype, not a complete messaging system. It runs against a fixed, scripted conversation rather than a real backend, has one animation style rather than a range of options, and hasn't been through the kind of device, performance, or accessibility testing a shipped feature would need. It exists to demonstrate the core idea clearly, not to serve as a finished implementation.

Where this could go

A product and engineering team could take this in several directions: different animation styles or intensities, a lighter treatment for rapid-fire messages, personalization tied to a recipient or a relationship, deeper accessibility work, and integration with an existing messaging architecture rather than a scripted demo. The version here is meant as a starting point for that conversation, not a proposal to ship as-is.
